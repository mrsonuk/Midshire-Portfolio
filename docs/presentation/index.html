1 · The problem

Why emergency departments jam — the 60-second version

  • Patients arrive in a daily wave (quiet at 4am, peak early afternoon) — but many EDs staff a flat rota.
  • The sickest must be seen first (acuity), so lower-acuity patients queue — some give up and walk out unseen.
  • The biggest cause of gridlock isn't inside the ED at all: when the hospital's wards are full, admitted patients can't move out of the ED. They "board" in cubicles. That's called exit-block.
  • Blocked cubicles mean ambulances can't hand over → queues at the door.
The 4-hour standard
% of patients admitted, transferred or discharged within 4 hours. Constitutional target 95%; interim national target 78%. Most trusts miss it.
Decisions made blind
"Add a ward? Open SDEC? Change the rota?" — today these are argued from spreadsheets and instinct. Each choice costs £100k–£2m.
You can't A/B-test an emergency department on real patients. So we built one you can.

2 · The blueprint

The NHS "Model Emergency Department"

NHS England's blueprint (Feb 2026) for how a high-performing ED should be laid out and run. It organises the department by how sick you are × how long you've been there, and names the building blocks a good ED should have:

Front door (first 15 min)
Streaming nurse decides where you go. RAT — a senior doctor sees the sickest immediately. Ambulance handover within 15–45 min.
Core ED (0–4 h)
Majors cubicles, resus, minor injuries (UTC), a seated "fit-to-sit" area, a children's area (PAU) — see, treat, decide.
Admission-avoiders (same day)
SDEC, EEMAC, CDU, frailty unit, mental-health liaison — treat and send home the patients who would otherwise be admitted to a scarce bed.

Most trusts have some of these blocks. The question the product answers: which missing block is worth building, for this specific department, and what is it worth in performance and pounds?

3 · The product

What we built: a digital twin, in plain clothes

A digital twin = a working computer copy of your department. Simulated patients arrive in the same daily wave as yours, queue for the same cubicles and clinicians, and leave through the same routes — so you can change anything and watch what happens, safely.

  • Flow-first & plain-language — the user sees queues, pinch points, standards, pounds. All simulation jargon is hidden.
  • One saved department — set it up once; every screen (ops, exec, planner, analyst) models the same ED and agrees with the others.
  • Offline by design — no cloud calls; runs on locked-down NHS networks.
  • Every assumption visible and editable — no black box.

4 · How the engine works

Under the bonnet: two simple ideas, joined

Idea 1 — simulate every patient (the "DES")
Like a flight simulator for the department: thousands of individual virtual patients arrive, queue, get treated, move on. Great at queues, staffing and "who waits where".
Idea 2 — simulate the hospital around it (the "System Dynamics")
The wards, mental-health beds and community services as filling-and-draining tanks. Great at the big slow pressure that actually causes gridlock.
Why both? Because the worst ED problem — exit-block — is caused outside the ED. A patient-only simulator literally cannot see it. A tanks-only model can't see queues or rotas. Ours couples the two, every simulated hour.

On top of that: Monte-Carlo (run many weeks, because no two weeks are alike) and validation (prove the simulated journeys follow the real clinical pathway). The next four slides take each in turn.

4 · How the engine works — the patient simulator

DES in plain English: a supermarket with triage

Discrete-Event Simulation (DES): create virtual patients one at a time and walk each through the department, minute by minute. Every queue forms for the same reason a supermarket queue does — more people than open tills. Except here:

  • The sickest jump the queue. Every resource serves by clinical priority (acuity 1 first) — exactly as an ED does.
  • A cubicle is held, not borrowed. A patient keeps their cubicle across X-ray and review until they leave — meanwhile doctors and nurses are borrowed stage by stage. Both are contended at once.
  • Staffing varies by hour. The rota is the model: 3 doctors at 4am, 14 at 2pm.
  • People give up. If the wait exceeds a patient's patience they walk out unseen (LWBS) — long queues create invisible clinical risk, and the model shows it.
  • Ambulances are physical. A crew occupies a handover bay until the patient is accepted — crowding queues real vehicles.

What a virtual patient looks like

Arrives 14:07, by ambulance · acuity 2
→ handover bay 12 min
→ streaming nurse 6 min
→ RAT senior review 15 min
→ cubicle acquired · bloods, imaging
→ decision: admit
→ waits for a ward bed… (this wait depends on the whole hospital — next slide)
→ transfer · cubicle released · LOS 6h 40m

The pathway itself — which stages exist and how they connect — is a faithful copy of the NHS Model ED design, stored as data and validated.

4 · How the engine works — the hospital around the ED

System Dynamics in plain English: the bathtub

Think of the hospital's ward beds as a bathtub: admissions pour in from the top (from the ED and from everywhere else), discharges drain out of the bottom. The water level is bed occupancy.

  • NHS hospitals run at ~92% full. Above that, finding a bed takes longer and longer — the tub is nearly overflowing.
  • Our model gives the tub realistic plumbing: a steady background inflow (elective & other admissions) and a drain that speeds up or slows down with how full it is.
  • Two more tanks: mental-health beds and community / virtual-ward capacity — spare community capacity actively pulls patients out of hospital, draining the tub faster.
The feedback loop: tub nearly full → ward bed waits stretch → admitted patients board in ED cubicles → cubicles blocked → queues everywhere → ambulances stack at the door.

That chain is exit-block, and in our model it emerges from the physics — nobody typed it in.
270 → 595 min
Admitted patients' time in the ED, feedback off vs on — the model reproduces exit-block from the bed stock alone. Switch feedback off and you get the naïve model back.

4 · How the engine works — the handshake

How the two models talk: an hourly handshake

Patient simulator (DES)
runs freely for one simulated hour
counting admissions, discharges,
diversions, ED census
⇄
Hospital model (SD)
absorbs that hour's flows, updates the tanks,
publishes fresh signals:
bed-wait multiplier · community pull · crowding
EVERY SIMULATED HOUR
1 · Drain
The hour's patient flows are handed from the DES to the hospital model.
2 · Update
The tanks integrate: occupancy moves, the bed-wait multiplier is recomputed and smoothed.
3 · Publish
The new signals are pinned for the next hour; the DES reads them (e.g. bed waits stretch ×2.5) but can't disturb them mid-hour.

For the engineers: this is a zero-order-hold co-simulation — hold signals constant between exchanges, use smooth bounded feedback plus first-order smoothing, and the loop is provably stable (we test for oscillation in CI).

4 · How the engine works — uncertainty

Monte-Carlo: because no two weeks are the same

One simulated week is one roll of the dice — a lucky week or a horrible one. So we run the same department many independent weeks (different random arrivals each time) and report the spread.

  • Every standard gets a 95% confidence interval, not a point estimate.
  • The product shows it as good / typical / tough week — language any board understands.
  • Wide spread is itself a finding: "plan to the lower bound".
"4-hour: 78%"
what a one-run tool tells you
"Typical 78% · good 85% · tough 71%"
what we tell you — across 24 independent simulated weeks
The recommendation engine goes further: it tests each candidate change with paired weeks (same luck, with and without the change) and only recommends when the improvement is statistically real. Silence over noise.

5 · Trust & validation

How we know it isn't making things up

1 · Clinical conformance
Every simulated patient's journey is checked, step by step, against the intended Model ED pathway graph. A clean run passes 100%; deliberately injected errors are caught. The pathway can't silently drift from the clinical design.
2 · Emergent physics
Exit-block, walk-outs under starvation, priority for the sickest, cubicles capped by nursing — these behaviours emerge from the model and are asserted in CI on every change, not hand-tuned in.
3 · A real trust
We fed in Barnsley's published figures — 290 attendances/day, 91.8% bed occupancy — and nothing else. The model produced 71.3% on the 4-hour standard. Barnsley's real reported figure: 67.5%. Within 4 points, untuned.
Observed performance (like that 67.5%) is never fed in as an input — it is held back and used only to test the model. That separation is what makes the validation honest.

Plus ~38 end-to-end checks run in CI on every code change — the test suite is the contract.

7 · Jargon buster

Every term we've used, in one place

4-hour standard — % of patients admitted, transferred or discharged within 4 h of arrival. Constitutional 95%; interim 78%.
Acuity — how sick you are, 1 (life-threatening) to 5 (minor). Drives priority.
Exit-block — full wards stop the ED admitting; patients board in cubicles and everything backs up.
LWBS — left without being seen: gave up waiting and walked out. Hidden clinical risk.
Boarding — an admitted patient stuck in the ED waiting for a ward bed.
Handover — transferring an ambulance patient to ED care; standard is ≤45 min, crews are stuck until it happens.
RAT — Rapid Assessment & Treatment: a senior doctor at the front door for the sickest arrivals.
UTC — Urgent Treatment Centre: minor illness/injury stream alongside the ED.
SDEC — Same-Day Emergency Care: specialty assessment that sends patients home the same day instead of admitting.
EEMAC — extended emergency ambulatory care (up to 12 h) for patients expected to go home.
CDU — Clinical Decision Unit: short-stay observation beds avoiding a ward admission.
Frailty unit (FAU) — early assessment for frail older patients, preventing unnecessary admission.
MH liaison — the mental-health team in the ED; 1-hour response standard.
ECDS — Emergency Care Data Set: the national record of every ED attendance; our calibration format.
DES — Discrete-Event Simulation: modelling every patient individually through queues and resources.
System Dynamics (SD) — modelling the hospital as filling/draining stocks (the bathtub) with feedback.
Digital twin — a working computer copy of a real system you can safely experiment on.
Monte-Carlo — running the model many times with different random draws to measure the spread of outcomes.
Confidence interval — the range results genuinely fall in (our good week / tough week).
Conformance — the automated check that every simulated journey follows the intended clinical pathway.

7 · Straight answers

What it is not (yet) — the honesty slide

  • It is a planning and design tool, not a live operational system — no real-time feed yet (the intraday "today" mode is the designed hook for an EPR/FHIR feed later).
  • Out of the box it runs on synthetic, clearly-flagged parameters — treat results as indicative until calibrated and validated against the trust's own data.
  • Stage-level treatment times are assumptions (no national dataset records them); they are editable and validated against observed outcomes, but they are not measurements.
  • Single department, no login today; multi-site and auth are roadmap.
  • Governance for real-data pilots (DPIA, clinical-safety case) is not started — required before any deployment with patient-level data.
Our position: every number carries its assumptions, every assumption is editable, and the model must earn trust by reproducing a trust's own observed performance before anyone acts on it.
